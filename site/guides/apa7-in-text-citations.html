APA 7 in-text citations, decided in one page

The reference list gets all the attention. In-text citations are where most supervisor comments actually land. Here is every pattern you will need, with the two decisions that cause almost all the errors.

The two forms: parenthetical and narrative

Every in-text citation is either parenthetical — author and year inside brackets at the end of the sentence — or narrative, where the author is part of your sentence and only the year sits in brackets. The meaning is identical. The choice is about what you want the reader to notice.

Parenthetical: Dynamic capabilities shape performance over time (Smith, 2024). Narrative: Smith (2024) argues that dynamic capabilities shape performance over time.

Use the narrative form when the researcher is the point of the sentence; use the parenthetical form when the claim is the point. In a literature review, alternating the two keeps the prose from becoming a list of names at the end of every sentence. What you must not do is mix the punctuation: narrative citations take a comma before the year only when the author element ends in a possessive or a suffix; otherwise the year follows the names directly.

One rule that surprises people: in the narrative form, two authors are joined with "and", not the ampersand. In the parenthetical form they are joined with &. That is the only place in APA 7 where the connector changes.

Narrative: Smith and Lee (2024) found... Parenthetical: ...was found (Smith & Lee, 2024).

The et al. rule changed in APA 7

This is the single most common carry-over error from APA 6. Under the sixth edition you listed every author up to five, then used et al. from the sixth. APA 7 uses et al. from the first citation whenever there are three or more authors. A 20-author paper is cited as (Smith et al., 2024) on first mention, even though the reference list below enumerates all twenty.

Two details that get marked: et al. has a period after al. but not after et; and there is a comma between the author element and the year, so (Smith et al., 2024) — not (Smith et al. 2024). In the narrative form the same rule applies: Smith et al. (2024) found...

The reference list behaves differently. There, et al. never appears. Twenty or fewer authors are all named; with 21 or more you list the first nineteen, an ellipsis, then the final author. If your in-text citation says et al. and your reference entry also says et al., one of them is wrong.

Group authors and abbreviations

Organisations are authors in their own right. Spell the name in full on first use, and introduce a short form in square brackets if you intend to use it later. After that first citation, the short form alone is enough.

First: The guidance was issued by the American Psychological Association (APA, 2020). or: (American Psychological Association [APA], 2020) Later: ...as APA (2020) recommends. / ...as recommended (APA, 2020).

Do not invent an abbreviation that could be confused with a person's surname, and do not abbreviate a group name that is already short. "The World Bank" is fine as written; turning it into (WB, 2024) makes the reader work harder for no gain. If two of your sources share the same abbreviation, spell both out every time.

Same author, same year

When one author has two works from the same year, append lowercase letters in alphabetical order of the reference titles: 2020a, 2020b. The letters are assigned by title, not by publication month or by the order you read them. Once assigned, they must match in the text and in the reference list, and the suffixes attach to the year inside the same parentheses.

Reference list: Smith, J. A. (2024a). Adaptive capacity in firms. Journal of Management. Smith, J. A. (2024b). Dynamic capabilities revisited. Organization Studies. In text: (Smith, 2024a, 2024b) <- both works support one claim Smith (2024b) disagrees... <- one specific work

Do not add a letter to a single work just because another year is close. 2023 and 2024 need no letter. The letters only disambiguate an exact year collision for the same author or same author team.

No author, no date, in press

If a work genuinely has no author, move the title into the author position. In the reference list a title that would normally take sentence case keeps it; in the in-text citation, use a shortened version in double quotation marks with the year. A corporate author is not "no author" — an organisation named on the source counts.

( "Dynamic Capabilities in Practice," 2024 )

When no publication date can be found, use (n.d.) — lowercase, with periods, inside the parentheses. Use it only after you have actually looked. (in press) is reserved for a work that has been accepted for publication but not yet assigned a final date; it is not a synonym for "submitted".

Direct quotations need a location

A paraphrase needs only author and year. A direct quotation also needs a precise location, so the reader can find the exact words. For print sources give a page number. For sources without pages, give a paragraph number, a section heading, or a timestamp for audio and video.

(Smith, 2024, p. 45) (Smith, 2024, para. 7) (Smith, 2024, "Methods" section, para. 2) (Smith, 2024, 14:32)

Use p. for one page and pp. for a range. In narrative form the page number follows the closing parenthesis of the year: Smith (2024, p. 45) describes... A quotation of 40 words or more becomes a block quote: start on a new line, indent the whole block by 0.5 inch, double-space it, omit the surrounding quotation marks, and place the parenthetical citation after the final punctuation.

Multiple works in one parenthetical

When several sources support a single claim, put them inside one set of parentheses, separated by semicolons, and order them alphabetically by first author's surname — the same order as your reference list. Do not sort by year or by importance.

Dynamic capabilities predict performance across contexts (Chen, 2022; Lee & Wang, 2023; Smith et al., 2024).

If two works by the same author appear in that set, the later years follow the earlier ones and the author name is not repeated: (Smith, 2020, 2024a, 2024b).

Personal communications and secondary sources

Emails, interviews you conducted, phone calls and private messages are personal communications. They appear in the text only, with the communicator's initial and surname, an exact date, and no entry in the reference list, because the reader cannot retrieve them.

(A. Smith, personal communication, March 14, 2026)

If you read a work quoted inside another work and could not access the original, cite the source you actually read and say so: (as cited in Lee, 2023, p. 112). The reference list contains only Lee. Track these down where you can — a chain of secondary citations is a common examiner question in a doctoral viva.

Pre-submission check

  1. Every claim that is not common knowledge carries a citation.
  2. Every in-text citation resolves to exactly one reference-list entry, and vice versa.
  3. Three-or-more-author works use et al. from the first mention, with the period.
  4. Narrative citations say "and"; parenthetical citations say &.
  5. Every direct quotation has a page, paragraph, section or timestamp.
  6. Block quotes of 40+ words are indented, unquoted, and cited after the final period.
  7. Same-author-same-year suffixes match between text and list.
  8. Multiple works share one set of parentheses, alphabetised, semicolon-separated.
  9. Personal communications appear in text only, with an exact date.

The reference list has its own rule set — sentence case, italics, DOIs, the 21-author ellipsis. Read The APA 7 reference list, checked line by line for that half, or run a pasted list through the reference list auditor for the mechanical errors.
